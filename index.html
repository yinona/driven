Computational Materials Physics of Driven Systems

We study metals driven far from equilibrium by intense electric fields, irradiation, or severe mechanical deformation. The method is the same across these drives: isolate the controlling microscopic mechanism with atomistic simulation, expose the scaling by varying the drive, and write a reduced model that can be tested in experiment.

Since 2013 the group has led the Hebrew University collaboration with CERN on material limits of high-gradient accelerating structures for the Compact Linear Collider (CLIC).

CERN · CLIC collaboration

Vacuum breakdown in high-gradient accelerators

Vacuum breakdown — the sudden transition of a vacuum gap from insulating to conducting — limits the electric field that copper accelerating structures can sustain. Conventional models do not account for the observed material dependence, which tracks crystal structure rather than bulk thermodynamics, or for the large statistical variability of breakdown.

The group’s working hypothesis is that the controlling mechanism is the plastic response of the electrode, specifically the dynamics of mobile dislocations. The Mobile Dislocation Density Fluctuation (MDDF) model treats those dislocations as a birth–death process whose fluctuations can nucleate breakdown. It predicts the field dependence of the breakdown rate and accounts for pre-breakdown signals such as dark-current spikes.

These predictions are tested with collaborators at CERN: field-emission statistics, cryogenic high-field tests, and TEM cross-sections of conditioned copper cathodes. A review with the principal CLIC collaborators appeared in Reviews of Modern Physics (2026). The group co-organizes the MeVArc workshop series (chair, 2017).

Research

Three ideas recur: mean-field reductions that yield scaling laws; effective temperatures that map a driven steady state onto equilibrium thermodynamics; and stochastic transitions in an internal population (mobile dislocations, point defects) that set thresholds such as breakdown or a creep crossover.

High-field electrodes

Stochastic dislocation models of vacuum breakdown, conditioning as cyclic restructuring of the near-surface network, and electron microscopy of field-exposed copper. Primary experimental partner: CERN / CLIC.

Irradiation-driven creep

In nanostructured alloys, grain boundaries dominate defect absorption. Grain-boundary flow under a sustained defect flux produces irradiation creep whose grain-size dependence marks the crossover from boundary-dominated to bulk-dominated response.

Phase evolution under shear

Severe plastic deformation drives immiscible alloys into steady states described by an effective temperature. Crystal structure sets the mixing mechanism: dislocations cross FCC interfaces (superdiffusive mixing) and are blocked at BCC ones (diffusive mixing).

Liquids and learned state variables

Crystallization kinetics at deep undercooling, a predicted liquid–liquid transition in iron at core–mantle pressures, and a funded study of vibrational entropy in liquids using machine-learned estimators (HUJI AI initiative, 2026–2027).
