Racah Institute of Physics · Interdisciplinary Computational Physics Laboratory · Center for Nanoscience and Nanotechnology · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

We study how metals respond when they are driven far from equilibrium by irradiation, intense electric fields, or severe mechanical deformation. We isolate the controlling microscopic mechanism with atomistic simulation, vary the drive to expose scaling, and write a reduced model that we can test in experiment. Our lab work uses electron microscopy (SEM, EBSD, TEM) and focused-ion-beam cross-sectioning, including images of subsurface dislocation structures in high-field electrodes.

Our group is involved in the Hebrew University collaboration with CERN on the Compact Linear Collider (CLIC). We also work with R. S. Averback and P. Bellon at the University of Illinois on phase evolution in immiscible alloys under shear and irradiation.

CERN · CLIC collaboration

Vacuum breakdown in high-gradient accelerators

Vacuum breakdown is the sudden switch of a vacuum gap from insulating to conducting. It limits how hard one can push a high-gradient accelerator. Older models did not explain two facts we care about: the material dependence tracks crystal structure rather than bulk thermodynamics, and the breakdown statistics are broad.

We argue that the electrode’s plastic response, specifically dislocation dynamics, is what sets the limit. The stresses from the field are too small for deterministic failure. Our Mobile Dislocation Density Fluctuation (MDDF) model treats mobile dislocations as a birth–death process. Fluctuations in that population can nucleate breakdown. The model gives the field dependence of the breakdown rate, matches high-field scaling, and accounts for dark-current spikes before breakdown. It also says that conditioning is cyclic evolution of the near-surface dislocation network.

We test this with colleagues at CERN. Dark-current spike statistics match the model. The field-emission enhancement factor β fluctuates, as one would expect if dislocations rearrange the surface. Cryogenic tests raise the saturation field. TEM cross-sections of copper cathodes show dislocation-denuded zones in field-exposed regions; unexposed regions keep their original defect structure. The subsurface change is stronger at lower temperature. A review with the main CLIC partners is in Reviews of Modern Physics (2026). We help organize the MeVArc workshop series.

Averback · Bellon collaboration

Driven multiphase alloys

Together with R. S. Averback and P. Bellon at Illinois, we use matched experiments and simulations to watch how phase stability and microstructure evolve under sustained shear or irradiation.

In immiscible alloys, steady driving produces a steady state that one can describe with an effective temperature set by the deformation path. Crystal structure sets the mixing mechanism. Dislocations cross FCC interfaces (superdiffusive mixing) and stop at BCC ones (diffusive mixing). That is why shear-driven transport can outrun thermal diffusion by a large factor. In a ternary alloy, the measured steady-state compositions followed the lever rule at the fitted effective temperature. Coherent interfaces let dislocations through; incoherent ones block them. The effective temperature scales with the shear modulus, and ternary grain-boundary segregation raises it further.

From our papers

Research

Three ideas keep coming back in our work. Mean-field reductions give scaling laws for things one can measure. Effective temperatures map a driven steady state onto equilibrium thermodynamics. And transitions in an internal population — mobile dislocations or point defects — set thresholds such as breakdown and creep crossovers.

High-field electrodes

We model vacuum breakdown with MDDF, treat conditioning as cyclic restructuring of the near-surface dislocation network, and use TEM to look for dislocation-denuded zones in field-exposed copper. Our experimental partners are at CERN on CLIC.

Shear-driven phase evolution

With Averback and Bellon we study immiscible alloys under severe plastic deformation: superdiffusive versus diffusive mixing, set by crystal structure, and an effective temperature that recovers the lever rule. Interface coherence decides whether dislocations can cross.

Irradiation-driven creep

Grain boundaries are the main sinks for radiation-induced point defects. A sustained defect flux drives grain-boundary flow, which shows up as irradiation creep. Grain size sets whether boundaries or the bulk dominate absorption.

Liquids and learned state variables

We work on crystallization at deep undercooling and on a predicted liquid–liquid transition in iron at core–mantle pressures (LHCP and LFCC). A funded study (HUJI AI initiative, 2026–2027) develops an estimator of vibrational entropy in liquids, with R. Shikler and M. Krief.
